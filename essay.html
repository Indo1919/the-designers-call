The Designer’s Call

Matias Indacochea. Visual Culture Seminar, Parsons School of Design. 18 November 2026. 544 words, citing five selections from the collection.

Designers throughout industry are using AI every day now, and the central debate that has been happening in the design community, including in our classroom, has moved past whether we should be using AI. The question has shifted to how much is too much before designers start losing credibility and the product becomes slop. The conversation is shifting from which designer has the best visual eye to who has that and can also explain the problem-solving behind the approach and the reasoning behind the direction, while staying in the driver’s seat of the choices and output AI makes. This showcase of responses argues that AI should be treated as a tool that can supercharge UI/UX designers, as long as the key point is that the designer keeps the wheel. I built this product based on my analysis of my classmates' micro-responses because, together, they show both the case for that position and the doubts behind it.

Making choices sets the standard at the center; before AI came up, all the readings described design as mainly decision-making, which it is at its core. Crystal chose Karel Martens’ line “designing is making choices” and that “each choice always shuts out other possibilities” (Mr 01). Leila referenced Rauno Freiberg, who argues that explaining why something feels right and the thoughts behind it “helps nurture taste” (Mr 08). With these two pieces of evidence together, it shows that taste was never just about having a good eye for something; it has always been the reasoning behind the choices that were made, and that is exactly the skill AI makes more crucial for anyone. Felix visualizes this perspective by comparing AI to a smartphone camera: everyone has one, but “not everybody suddenly became an expert,” and the tool can still “supercharge the work” (Mr 06). This is the position I align with the most, as AI does not completely replace the judgement of a good designer whatsoever; it just gives more time for exploration and evolution for that judgement to make a better product. The main condition is control, as Andrea argues that designers need to maintain AI as a tool “rather than letting it make the decisions for us” (Mr 16). This is what I mean by keeping control of the driver’s seat or steering the ship: AI can take part in the execution, but the designer must lay out the decision-making throughout the process and the direction itself. On the other hand, as in the real world, doubt also enters the room in conversations about this topic, rightfully so. Valerie argues that when a short prompt makes a complete essay, “the opportunity for growth was robbed from you” (Mr 23). This context is crucial to keep central, as using AI comes with responsibility and restraint, and we must maintain a clear, correct direction as we explore this new technology. Using AI means giving the responsibility to yourself as a designer to protect and restrict those parts that could be skipped for a more efficient outcome and knowing that they are very important parts of the design process and they should not be skipped, but instead supercharged with this tool, if needed. It is in our hands, our craft, and an immense responsibility, especially when using this technology.
